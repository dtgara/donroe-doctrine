Base case (50%): the compensation demand functions as a negotiating lever rather than a deal-breaker, consistent with a pattern in which Trump has attached late conditions to near-final frameworks before accepting terms close to the original draft. Oman continues shuttling between Washington and Tehran, and a shipping-management framework is signed within the watch period, though the routing-authority question Secretary Rubio has flagged — that Iran gain no more control over the Strait than it held before the February 28 strikes — remains unresolved going into any signing.

Downside case (35%): Iran's negotiators treat this week's demand as confirmation that Washington's stated openness is not sincere, and the chief negotiator's "theater diplomacy" framing hardens into Tehran's operating assumption, repeating the pattern of June's memorandum of understanding, which collapsed within weeks over exactly the same routing-authority question now resurfacing. Oil holds range-bound in the high $70s to mid $80s as traders discount the standoff continuing, and global schedule reliability, already at a post-2022 low, degrades further into the third quarter.

Tail case (15%): the compensation figure is made specific enough — a number, not a category — that it becomes a poison pill neither government can accept without domestic cost, ending this negotiating round and reviving the naval-escort posture that preceded June's MOU. That outcome would move this issue's entire Sector Impact grid, not only Energy, since the Logistics and Agriculture entries above both assume continued friction rather than a full closure.

Key assumption: that "compensation" is being used as a lever rather than a literal precondition, an assumption untested until Washington attaches a number to it. The indicator worth watching is not the rhetoric exchanged this week but whether Oman's foreign ministry, the most reliable barometer of actual movement all month, describes the talks as "continuing" or "paused" once this round of statements settles.
