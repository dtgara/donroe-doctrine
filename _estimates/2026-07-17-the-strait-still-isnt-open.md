Base case (55%): the exchange continues on its established cadence — Iranian attacks on tankers, US strikes on Iranian command, air-defense, and coastal surveillance sites — without broadening into a wider regional war, consistent with six consecutive days of strikes through July 16. Brent crude's rise, 13 percent this week and roughly 40 percent since the February escalation, reads as the market pricing a continuation of the pattern rather than a break from it. Downside case (30%): Iran declines to issue the public assurance Washington says it is waiting for before nuclear talks can resume, war-risk insurance — already running 2 to 6 percent of vessel value, against a fraction of a percent before the conflict — keeps climbing until shipowners route around the Strait regardless of what either government calls open, and Gulf producers extend the caution already visible in Aramco's largest monthly price cut since 2000 and Qatar's paused LNG buildout. Tail case (15%): a strike reaches a Gulf state's own naval or air assets rather than commercial shipping, converting a US-Iran exchange fought near the Gulf into a Gulf war — a scenario that would require repricing the doctrine's own regional financial commitments, including the Saudi energy fund and DataVolt's $20 billion in US data-center investment, rather than merely the Strait itself.

Key assumption: that Gulf capitals continue to treat the strikes as an American problem occurring near their coastline rather than a war drawing them in — an assumption the insurance market is already pricing more cautiously than either government's public statements. The indicator worth watching is not the strike count, which has become routine, but whether a Gulf state's own military assets engage directly: the threshold between strikes near the Gulf and a war in it.
