Base case (60%): the blockade of Iranian ports and U.S. tanker escorts continue through the Shenzhen meetings without a negotiated settlement or renewed strikes, and Brent trades between $85 and $100. On November 12 the President wrote that "We don't need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed," adding that the United States has "their money" and "their Strait."[^1] Two days earlier he had described renewed military action as "quite unlikely" for now.[^1] Brent reached a two-month low on November 9 as Gulf output recovered and traded near $92 the following day.[^2]

Downside case (30%): attacks on shipping continue at October's rate, when 37 vessels were struck, and a mine or projectile strike closes a lane long enough to reverse the recovery in Gulf exports.[^2] Iran's military said four tankers were hit on November 12 after ignoring warnings.[^3] Brent would return above $105, where it traded in mid-October.

Tail case (10%): direct talks resume and produce a framework before year-end, placing Brent below $85. The Wall Street Journal reported on November 11 that the Vice President was weighing a meeting with a senior Iranian security official. On November 12 the President directed him to give "his complete and total attention" to corruption in Democratic-led states.[^3]

Key assumption: the administration treats the blockade as a substitute for an agreement rather than as leverage toward one, and the Democratic majorities seated in January do not change that calculation. The President has said he will become "Mr. Veto."[^4] The election result is assessed with high confidence not to have involved the President directly, per his statement that "I WAS NOT ON THE BALLOT."[^5] The view changes on a confirmed meeting between the Vice President and an Iranian official, or on a resumption of U.S. strikes, which an unnamed CENTCOM official described on November 13 as "pure speculation."[^6]
