Base case (60%): a second-phase Section 232 semiconductor action is issued this autumn carrying an investment-linked exemption, and Samsung and SK hynix do not announce new US fabs before it takes effect. Neither has announced a US chip plant since January 2025, and the capital required to reach parity with Taiwanese commitments is large enough that the near-term rational response is to absorb the duty and negotiate.

Downside case (25%): the exemption is written against announced commitments rather than operating capacity. The polysilicon proclamation of August 6 already conditions relief on a commitment to build, which makes the tariff a mechanism for collecting investment pledges rather than revenue. Pledges are cheap to make, slow to verify, and difficult to withdraw from publicly — the enforcement problem arrives in 2028, not this year.

Tail case (15%): Seoul treats the action as inconsistent with the existing bilateral arrangement and reopens it. This is unlikely while Korean firms are still seeking exemption on individual terms, which is the outcome the exemption structure is designed to produce.

Key assumption: the tariff is an instrument for extracting capital commitments, and the rate is set at whatever level makes the commitment cheaper than the duty. Last week's indicator has now reported. The September 8 Canadian list was published broader than announced — $27.6 billion, with plastics added — rather than narrower, which on the stated test means Ottawa is not pricing in a negotiated exit. The indicator for this week's assumption is the text of the proclamation itself: an exemption keyed to capital committed would confirm it, while one keyed to wafers actually produced on US soil would not.
