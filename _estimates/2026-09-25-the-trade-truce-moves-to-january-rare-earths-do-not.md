Base case (55%): the truce holds through January 10 without a comprehensive agreement, continuing the pattern set at Busan eleven months ago — incremental extensions in place of resolution. The September 24 meeting produced two additional months and little else; both governments announced their own gains separately rather than jointly, and neither rare earth licensing nor semiconductor export controls appear in either readout.

Downside case (25%): a single unresolved item forces the pace before the new deadline. Rare earth shipments to the US fell 20 percent month-over-month in August, a pattern consistent with Beijing holding licensing as leverage rather than easing it as the original Busan terms implied; a further tightening, timed to the pending Taiwan arms package still on hold from the prior round, is the more probable trigger than a US-initiated escalation.

Tail case (20%): the additional two months produce the "larger trade agreement" Bessent described as the purpose of the extension, addressing tariff levels, rare earth licensing volumes, and agricultural enforcement in a single package before January.

Key assumption: both governments continue to value the appearance of stability — a state dinner, two pandas dispatched to the Atlanta Zoo, a tour of the ballroom under construction — over resolving the disputes that produced this week's market reaction, in which Chinese equities fell on the extension itself rather than on any deterioration. The assumption fails if September's rare earth export data, due in early October, shows shipment volumes recovering toward the pre-control baseline of roughly 620 tonnes a month; that would indicate the truce is being observed in substance and not only in form.
