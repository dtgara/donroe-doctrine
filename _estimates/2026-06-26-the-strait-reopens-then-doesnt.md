Base case (45%): this week's Strait of Hormuz incident is contained, and shipping activity gradually resumes over the following month, consistent with a slow, insurance-led normalization in which war-risk premiums step down gradually rather than a single snapback to pre-war rates. Under this reading, the attack that halted the evacuation plan is best understood as friction during a difficult handoff — from active conflict to monitored ceasefire — rather than as a rejection of the ceasefire itself. Downside case (40%): the incident is the first instance of a recurring pattern in which Iran uses intermittent, deniable disruption to retain practical leverage over the chokepoint without formally abandoning the memorandum it signed at Versailles — an outcome multiple shipping analysts have specifically flagged as more probable than either a full reopening or a full closure, on the logic that ambiguous control is more valuable to Tehran than either clear alternative. The insurers repricing this week's transits are, in effect, already voting for this case over the base case. Tail case (15%): the digital-services-tax dispute, still in its opening threat stage, escalates faster than the Hormuz situation stabilizes and becomes the dominant trade story of the summer, displacing the Iran ceasefire's economic aftermath from the news cycle without actually resolving it — a reminder that this newsletter's ordering of stories by prominence is not the same as an ordering by consequence.

Key assumption: that a ceasefire signed by two governments binds the actors who control a physical chokepoint between them. The confidence rating on this issue returns to Low because this week is the first direct evidence against that assumption since it was made — evidence that arrived, notably, in the strait itself rather than in any subsequent statement from either government.
