Base case (55%): the pause is converted into either a signed framework or a further extension before the duties bind. This is the third deadline in the Canada file since the July 20 proclamations, and the two prior ones produced movement in the deadline rather than movement in the tariff. Prime Minister Carney's description of the talks on Wednesday — "very intense and delicate," with "important work still to be done" — is the language of a negotiation being managed toward continuation, not one at the point of rupture.

Downside case (30%): the text is not ready by end of day and the 50 percent rate takes effect on the $20 billion list, most of it USMCA-compliant goods. The commercial damage in the first week would be modest relative to the headline, since energy, potash, fish and critical minerals are all carved out; the durable cost is to USMCA itself, which Section 338 was invoked specifically to override, and which comes up for joint review next year.

Tail case (15%): a deal is signed and then challenged. Section 338 has never been used to impose duties, and a signed agreement resting on an untested 1930 authority would leave both the concessions and the leverage that produced them contingent on litigation the administration has already lost once this year, in the February IEEPA decision.

Key assumption: that the tariff is functioning as a deadline-generating device rather than as a revenue instrument — that the object is the extension, not the collection. The assumption is testable. The indicator is not the rhetoric around Keystone XL, which costs nothing to offer, but whether the published text specifies effective dates and product-level rates for the steel, aluminum and auto reductions, or whether it commits only to a process. A framework that announces alignment without schedules would indicate the deadline itself remains the deliverable.
