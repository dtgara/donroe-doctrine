Base case (50%): both ceasefires hold through the summer in modified form, consistent with a recurring pattern in which the doctrine's diplomatic wins are real but narrower than their announcements — a memorandum, not a treaty; a pause, not a resolution; a photograph of a signing, not a verified mechanism for enforcing what was signed. The Lebanon ceasefire's structure, brokered jointly with Qatar and Iran rather than by the United States alone, gives it more institutional backing than the June 11 announcement had, which may partly explain why it has drawn less immediate skepticism this week despite following directly on the heels of a failed one. Downside case (35%): one or both arrangements unravel within weeks, echoing the June 11 announcement that preceded a fresh round of Israeli strikes by less than a day — a precedent this newsletter is required by its own rules to take seriously rather than treat as resolved history, given that it is seven days old. Shipping and insurance markets, discussed below, are already behaving as though this case deserves real weight. Tail case (15%): both hold and expand into something closer to durable settlements, and the administration's evident preference for personally brokered, ceremonially staged agreements — a Versailles state dinner, a Doha back channel — is vindicated as a genuine alternative to slower multilateral process, rather than a substitute for the verification mechanisms multilateral process is usually built to provide.

Key assumption: that an agreement's durability can be assessed independently of the venue and stagecraft used to announce it. This assessment treats the Versailles setting as color, not signal — an assumption the doctrine's own track record this month gives some reason to hold loosely, given how much of the diplomacy under review has been staged for effect first and verified for substance second, if at all.
