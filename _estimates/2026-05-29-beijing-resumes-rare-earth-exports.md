Base case (60%): the rare earth and magnet export resumption announced this month holds through the summer, consistent with Beijing's established practice of using the restriction as leverage rather than settled policy — applied when useful, lifted when a larger deal is within reach. The May summit's framing supports this reading: the commitment was presented as a resumption tied to the broader trade relationship, not a unilateral concession, which gives Beijing every reason to let it run as long as the relationship stays profitable to maintain. Downside case (30%): the resumption proves conditional on an unstated concession — most plausibly related to the chip licensing framework or the Section 301 forced-labor tariffs still under consideration — that surfaces later and restarts the restriction cycle that already interrupted automotive, robotics, and defense production once this year. The precedent is recent enough that supply chain planners are not treating the current calm as durable; automotive and defense manufacturers who absorbed the first restriction have not meaningfully drawn down the buffer stock they built during it. Tail case (10%): the Court of International Trade's May 7 finding that the administration's 10 percent global tariff exceeds its statutory authority is upheld on appeal, unwinding a tariff the administration has treated, in the interim, as a fixed and permanent feature of the trade landscape rather than a contested one.

Key assumption: that a trade relationship conducted through sequential restriction-and-resumption cycles is functionally different from one conducted through sequential threat-and-concession cycles. Both produce the same chart — periods of calm interrupted by periods of leverage, repeating on a schedule set by whichever side currently wants something from the other. Only the caption changes depending on which capital is issuing it. The indicator worth watching is not whether the resumption holds, which it likely will in the near term, but whether either side begins describing it as a right rather than a favor — the linguistic shift that, in this relationship, has reliably preceded its withdrawal.
