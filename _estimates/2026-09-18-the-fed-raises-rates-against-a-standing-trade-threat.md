Base case (60%): the embargo threat is not executed against any partner before year-end, and trade policy continues to run on the instruments already in use. The threat was made on September 4 and the Fed did the opposite on September 16 without producing a response beyond a post. The administration's revealed preference across fourteen months has been to adjust the scope and rate of existing measures rather than to open a new front, and the July deficit list — Mexico at $27.5 billion, Vietnam at $23.3 billion, China at $15.2 billion, the European Union at $8.9 billion — includes three parties with live negotiations the embargo would terminate.

Downside case (25%): a single deficit partner is named and an import prohibition is initiated against a narrow product set, on the Canadian template. This is the case that deserves more weight than the rhetoric suggests, because the instrument exists and has been exercised: Section 338 has moved from an untested 1930 provision to a signed prohibition with an effective date inside eight weeks. The constraint is not legal authority but the absence of a discrimination predicate against partners who have not retaliated.

Tail case (15%): the pressure transfers from trade to the Board itself. Trump retained his stated confidence in Warsh after the hike, which is the observable that argues against this.

Key assumption: the embargo language is a bid for monetary policy rather than a trade objective, and therefore does not survive contact with a Fed that has already declined. The assumption fails if an action is opened against a partner running a surplus but no retaliation, which would indicate the deficit itself is the predicate. The indicator for the coming week is narrow: whether the September 29 Canadian exclusions take effect unmodified, and whether any Section 338 proceeding is initiated against a country other than Canada.
