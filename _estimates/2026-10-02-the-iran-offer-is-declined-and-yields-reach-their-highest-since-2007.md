Base case (50%): the standoff over the Strait of Hormuz continues without a negotiated reopening, and the cost shows up in rates rather than in the strait. The President declined Iran's seven-day reopening offer, and Brent returned to roughly $107 within a session. With oil elevated, the ten-year Treasury yield at 5.21 percent and a hike priced for October 28, the inflation channel is now the most visible transmission of the blockade into the domestic economy.

Downside case (30%): the blockade and the rate path reinforce each other. A second consecutive Federal Reserve increase, set against consumer inflation expectations the University of Michigan survey placed at 4.6 percent, tightens financial conditions at the point where energy costs are already rising. The trigger is more likely a further oil move than a decision in Washington.

Tail case (20%): talks resume on the phased reopening reported September 24, the offer is revised to meet US conditions, and Brent falls back toward the $98 level reached on September 21. This case requires the administration to accept terms it declined this week.

On Canada, the President said on September 28 that he expects Canada to say "Sir, we are sorry" within three or four weeks.[^1] The import ban took effect the following day.

Key assumption: the administration continues to treat the blockade as leverage worth its inflation cost ahead of the November 3 midterm elections. The assumption fails if Brent closes below $100 for several consecutive sessions, or if either government publicly restates terms for the reopening; either would indicate the cost is being weighed differently than it was this week.

[^1]: Trump says he expects Canada trade deal within weeks as import ban looms, BNN Bloomberg, September 28, 2026.
