Base case (55%): the NATO summit produces further rhetorical commitment to the 5 percent target and an announced review process, without a binding enforcement mechanism the administration could not itself later waive if a different priority intervened — consistent with how the original Hague commitment was structured, as a shared target rather than a treaty obligation with penalties attached. This reading treats the pre-summit rhetoric, including the direct U.S.-Poland spending comparison, as pressure applied ahead of a negotiation rather than a preview of its outcome. Downside case (30%): the summit surfaces open disagreement between the United States and specific lagging allies, consistent with the ambassador's pre-summit warning, without resolving it — leaving the 5 percent target formally intact but practically contested heading into the back half of the year, an outcome that would cost the administration little publicly while leaving the underlying compliance question unresolved. Tail case (15%): the six-month Pentagon review of U.S. force posture in Europe, running quietly in parallel to the summit and easy to overlook against the louder spending argument, produces a drawdown announcement the summit itself was not designed to preview — which would make this week's spending rhetoric look, in hindsight, like cover for a decision made on separate grounds.

Key assumption: that a spending target agreed to under pressure one year ago remains politically load-bearing enough, a year later, for the same pressure to be reapplied rather than quietly renegotiated downward. The Gulf capital numbers released this same week are a useful control case for how the administration behaves when it isn't applying pressure at all: when the goal is attracting investment rather than enforcing compliance, the number arrives in a press release, without a summit, an ambassador's warning, or a comparison chart.
