Base case (55%): the three exclusion proclamations take effect on September 29 substantially as signed. The pattern since August has been adjustment of scope rather than retreat — the September 15 proclamations add product lines and subtract others without touching the rate — and the transition rule, which leaves goods already imported but not yet entered subject to the 50 percent duty rather than the ban, reads as drafted for implementation rather than as a negotiating placeholder.

Downside case (30%): a challenge is filed before the end of the month and it is the exclusion, not the duty, that draws it. An outright prohibition on importation is a harder remedy to defend than an ad valorem rate, this is the first use of Section 338 in the statute's history, and Canadian distillers and dairy exporters now have both standing and a date certain. Trade counsel have been expecting filings since August without producing one.

Tail case (15%): Bombardier announces US production before September 29 and the threat is withdrawn. The company's constraint is that final assembly relocates on a multi-year timeline, which does not fit inside a three-week window, and the political pressure is currently running through Kansas rather than through Montreal.

Key assumption: the instrument is being used to collect production commitments, and exclusion is the escalation applied when a duty fails to produce one. Last week's indicator has reported and supports this. The semiconductor exemption Lutnick described on September 3 is measured against planned production — 2.5 times during construction, 1.5 times after — rather than against wafers shipped, which is the capital-committed test rather than the capacity-operating one. The indicator for this week is narrower: whether the September 29 proclamations are modified before they take effect, and whether the first Section 338 complaint appears on the CIT docket before or after that date.
